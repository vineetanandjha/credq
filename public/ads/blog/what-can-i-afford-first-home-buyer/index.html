What Can I Afford as a First Home Buyer?

Learn how Australian lenders assess your income, expenses, and debts to determine your true borrowing capacity and total home purchasing budget.

Understanding Your True Buying Budget in Australia

When stepping into the Australian property market for the first time, determining what you can afford is the foundational first step. Many prospective buyers mistake their total savings for their property budget, or assume an online borrowing power estimator provides a guaranteed lending approval. In reality, your true purchasing capacity is determined by a combination of your maximum borrowing capacity assessed under Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA) guidelines, your genuine cash deposit, and the upfront transaction costs required to complete settlement.

Working with a dedicated first home buyer mortgage broker helps demystify how different Australian banks and non-bank lenders assess your profile. Every financial institution maintains unique credit policies, treatment of overtime or bonus income, and living expense assessments. Understanding these criteria empowers you to search for properties with confidence, avoiding the frustration of bidding beyond your realistic financial limit.

1. How Australian Lenders Assess Borrowing Capacity

Borrowing power is not simply a multiplier of your gross salary. Australian lenders apply strict serviceability calculations to evaluate whether you can comfortably manage mortgage repayments over a standard 30-year loan term without suffering financial hardship.

The APRA Serviceability Buffer

Under APRA regulatory framework rules, Australian authorized deposit-taking institutions (ADIs) must assess your ability to repay a residential mortgage at a test rate significantly higher than the actual product interest rate. Lenders apply a minimum serviceability buffer of 3.0 percentage points above the loan's principal and interest interest rate. For example, if you apply for a variable rate home loan at 6.10% p.a., the bank will test your monthly budget as if interest rates were 9.10% p.a. This assessment buffer protects both the lender and borrower against future interest rate increases, but it also restricts the maximum amount you can borrow.

Net Income Verification

Lenders calculate serviceability using your post-tax net monthly income. Gross salaries are adjusted for mandatory PAYG tax withholdings, Medicare levies, and higher education debt obligations such as HECS/HELP. Variable income components are assessed with varying degrees of conservatism:

  • Base Salary / Wages: Accepted at 100% of verified income, evidenced by recent payslips, payment summaries, or tax returns.
  • Overtime and Allowances: Commonly shaded to 80% of historical earnings, requiring a minimum 6 to 12-month consistent history with the same employer.
  • Bonuses and Commissions: Typically averaged over two years and shaded to 80%, depending on lender policy.
  • Self-Employed Income: Calculated using net profit from tax returns and Notice of Assessments averaged across two financial years.

2. The Role of Living Expenses and Debts

Your ongoing commitments directly offset the amount of income available for mortgage debt servicing. Australian responsible lending obligations require lenders to examine your declared living costs and existing liabilities in detail.

Household Expenditure Measure (HEM) vs Declared Expenses

Lenders review your last 3 to 6 months of bank statement transactions to categorize your living expenses into standard categories including utilities, groceries, insurance, transport, entertainment, and child care. Banks compare your self-declared monthly expenses against the Household Expenditure Measure (HEM) benchmark. Lenders will automatically insert whichever figure is higher into their serviceability calculator. Substantially under-declaring your discretionary spending will not artificially inflate your borrowing capacity, as the HEM floor applies automatically.

Impact of Liabilities, Credit Cards, and HECS Debt

Existing debt commitments have a disproportionately large impact on your serviceability calculation:

  • Credit Card Limits: Lenders assess credit card debt based on the total limit, not your balance. A $10,000 credit limit is typically assessed as an ongoing monthly liability of around 3% of the limit ($300/month), which can reduce your total borrowing capacity by approximately $45,000 to $50,000.
  • Personal & Car Loans: Monthly repayments on fixed personal debts directly dollar-for-dollar reduce your available monthly debt-servicing capacity.
  • HECS/HELP Debt: Compulsory HELP repayments reduce your net monthly take-home pay according to ATO income thresholds. Paying off a HELP balance prior to applying can noticeably boost serviceability for high-earning graduates.
  • Buy-Now-Pay-Later (BNPL): Facilities like Afterpay or Zip are treated as active liability lines and require clean payment records to prevent automated underwriting flags.

3. Budget Planning Beyond the Purchase Price

A common pitfall for first home buyers in Australia is allocating 100% of their liquid savings toward the property deposit. Doing so leaves zero cash reserves for unavoidable upfront purchasing costs or post-settlement emergencies.

Upfront Costs Checklist

  • Stamp Duty (Transfer Duty): State government tax based on property value. (Check concessions under state first home buyer schemes).
  • Conveyancing & Legal Fees: $1,500 to $2,500 for title searches, contract reviews, and settlement representation.
  • Building & Pest Inspections: $500 to $900 prior to exchanging unconditional contracts.
  • Lender Establishment & Valuation Fees: $0 to $600 depending on loan package.
  • Lenders Mortgage Insurance (LMI): Applicable if borrowing over 80% LVR without a government guarantee or parental guarantor.

4. Real-World Borrowing Scenarios for Australian Buyers

To visualize how these assessment factors translate into property purchasing budgets, consider these two representative scenarios based on typical Australian market conditions:

Scenario A: Single First Home Buyer in Western Sydney

  • Gross Annual Income: $95,000 PAYG base salary (no HECS debt).
  • Savings / Deposit: $85,000 total cash reserves.
  • Ongoing Liabilities: $5,000 credit card limit (zero balance).
  • Assessed Borrowing Capacity: ~$460,000 to $490,000 mortgage.
  • Purchasing Budget: Utilizing the First Home Guarantee scheme (5% deposit, 0% LMI) with NSW stamp duty exemption up to $800,000, this buyer can target property prices up to $500,000 (e.g., an entry-level modern apartment in Parramatta or Blacktown), using $25,000 for deposit, $5,000 for upfront costs, and retaining $55,000 as a cash emergency buffer or loan reduction.

Scenario B: De Facto Couple Buying in Greater Sydney

  • Combined Gross Income: $175,000 ($100,000 + $75,000 PAYG).
  • Savings / Deposit: $160,000 cash savings.
  • Ongoing Liabilities: $350/month car loan repayment ($18,000 balance remaining).
  • Assessed Borrowing Capacity: ~$820,000 to $870,000 mortgage.
  • Purchasing Budget: With a 15% deposit plus LMI or First Home Guarantee approval, their total property purchasing budget ranges between $950,000 and $1,000,000 for a townhouse or house in outer Western Sydney.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do Australian banks calculate borrowing power for first home buyers?

Lenders evaluate your gross taxable income, subtract tax, verified living expenses, and monthly debt obligations, then apply a 3.0% APRA serviceability buffer rate to ensure you can manage repayments if interest rates rise.

What is the Household Expenditure Measure (HEM)?

HEM is a standard benchmark used by Australian financial institutions to estimate baseline living expenses according to marital status, dependents, and location. Lenders compare your self-declared expenses against HEM and adopt whichever total is higher.

Does HECS/HELP debt reduce my borrowing capacity?

Yes. Compulsory HELP debt repayments lower your post-tax take-home pay, directly reducing your monthly serviceability calculation and reducing borrowing capacity by tens of thousands of dollars.

How much emergency buffer should I keep after buying?

We recommend retaining 3 to 6 months of essential living costs and mortgage repayments in an offset account or redraw facility to cover unexpected maintenance, medical costs, or income changes.
